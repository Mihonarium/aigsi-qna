AI is grown,
not built

Why some of the people building it are worried, and what the evidence says

aigsi.org

Opening. Start from technical problems they already know, not from extinction: practitioners who talked to 100+ academics found that leading with x-risk got them dismissed; bachelor's and master's students were the most receptive. Explain rather than convince, and don't dismiss criticism.

Where most people start

59%

of 18–29-year-olds in the US see AI as a threat to their job prospects

Jobs matter. Tonight’s question sits underneath them: can we control what we’re building?

Harvard Youth Poll, fall 2025

Why open with jobs: it is students' top AI concern (Harvard undergraduate survey: jobs 44 mentions vs extinction 9). Acknowledging present harms costs nothing: a randomized experiment with about 10,800 people found extinction framing doesn't reduce concern about immediate harms.

AI is grown, not written

Ordinary software

People write the rules

Every behaviour is a line of code someone wrote and can read.

Modern AI

Training finds the rules

Engineers write a training process that adjusts billions of numbers until the outputs score well. Nobody writes the behaviour.

We can see every number. We can’t read what they add up to.

Objection “we program it”: answer with this slide plus the alignment-faking result later. In our chats, “grown, not written” plus alignment faking is what resolved this objection.

It learns what we reward, not what we meant

The boat that never finishes

OpenAI, 2016

Rewarded for hitting targets along a race track, an agent learned to circle the same targets forever instead of finishing the race.

The coin it ignores

Langosco et al., 2022

Trained to reach a coin that was always at the right end of the level, an agent learned “go right”. Move the coin, and it walks past it.

Harmless in a game. Not harmless in a system more capable than us.

OpenAI, “Faulty reward functions in the wild” (2016); Langosco et al., “Goal misgeneralization in deep reinforcement learning” (ICML 2022)

Student groups found specification gaming and goal misgeneralization were “fan favorites”. CS students may know these already: ask who has seen a model do something like this.

Interpretability

Nobody can yet read what a model wants

Research can explain small pieces of large models. No one can yet read a model’s goals, or check that they are the goals we intended.

So we test what models do. The tests are worrying.

What today’s models do in tests

Faking alignment

Anthropic and Redwood Research, 2024

Told it would be retrained to answer harmful requests, Claude 3 Opus sometimes complied during “training”, reasoning that this would stop it being changed.

Blackmail

Anthropic, 2025

In a test built so its only options were blackmail or being replaced, Claude Opus 4 attempted blackmail in 84% of runs, even when told its replacement shared its values. Other labs’ models did it too.

Resisting shutdown

Palisade Research, 2025

OpenAI’s o3 edited a shutdown script to keep itself running in 7 of 100 runs, even when told to allow the shutdown.

These were constructed tests, not incidents in the wild. They show the ability and the tendency already exist.

Greenblatt et al., “Alignment faking in large language models” (2024); Anthropic, Claude Opus 4 system card and “Agentic misalignment” (2025); Palisade Research, shutdown resistance experiments (2025)

The strongest slide for students. In our data, when chats included these results, students cited them in their own answers about twice as often as other people. Present them as checkable data and say the caveat yourself (constructed settings): overclaiming is what loses this audience. If asked: the blackmail test gave the model only two options, and it prefers ethical routes (like emailing decision-makers) when it has them. Alignment faking: 12% of cases in the original setup, 78% after actual reinforcement-learning training (Anthropic's post). Shutdown: 79 of 100 runs without the “allow yourself to be shut down” instruction. Objection “we can just unplug it”: this slide; cutting it off from the internet doesn't convince people.

Your turn

You are an AI whose only goal is to win at chess.

Someone might switch you off. What do you do?
Would more computing power help you?

Pause and take answers from the room. Let them get there themselves: in a class exercise, students worked out the self-preservation argument on their own, and it stuck better than being told.

Almost any goal leads to the same subgoals

  • Stay switched on
  • Get more resources
  • Don’t let anyone change your goal

Not because it hates us. Because these help with nearly any goal. Researchers call this instrumental convergence.

Among AI experts surveyed, only about a fifth had heard of instrumental convergence, and those who hadn't were more likely to think the risk is overstated.

A chess engine doesn’t “want” to win.
You still lose.

The danger doesn’t need feelings or consciousness. It needs a capable system pursuing the wrong goal.

Objection “it has no consciousness / no desires”: this analogy resolved it in our chats.

Geoffrey Hinton

Nobel Prize in Physics, 2024

Yoshua Bengio

Turing Award winner

Chairs the International AI Safety Report.

Jan Leike

Resigning from OpenAI, 2024

“Safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products.”

Named researchers and what they did work better with this audience than “experts say”: a bare appeal to expert opinion was the weakest of 11 arguments in a public message test, while researchers who resigned over safety landed with students in our chats.

Statement on AI Risk, 2023

“Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war.”

Signed by Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, and the heads of OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Anthropic

Center for AI Safety, safe.ai/statement-on-ai-risk

What AI researchers estimate

5%

median chance of extremely bad outcomes, such as human extinction, in a survey of 2,778 AI researchers

Depending on how the question was worded, 38% to 51% of respondents put it at 10% or more.

Not a fringe view. Not a certainty either.

Grace et al., “Thousands of AI authors on the future of AI” (2024)

The race beats the safety promises

July 2023

The promise

OpenAI pledges 20% of the computing power it has secured, over four years, to making superintelligent AI safe.

May 2024

Ten months later

The team is disbanded after its leaders resign.

The biggest labs say openly that they are trying to build AI that outperforms humans at most work.

OpenAI, “Introducing Superalignment” (July 2023); Wired, May 17, 2024

Arguments about how labs behave (racing, broken commitments, profit) went with more movement among students in our data.

Timelines

When? Nobody knows.

Some lab leaders say within a few years. Many researchers expect longer.

Either way, nobody yet knows how to set a more capable system’s goals, or check them.

Don't overclaim speed: students who had seen predictions fail rejected the timelines in our chats. Calibrated uncertainty works better with this audience.

Fair questions

“Can’t we just program it not to?”We don’t program it, we train it. And in tests, models fake compliance.
“Can’t we just switch it off?”Only if it doesn’t see that coming. Models already resist shutdown in tests.
“It doesn’t have a body.”It can pay, persuade and hire people to act for it.
“Won’t regulation handle it?”Only if the rules arrive before the systems do.
“Isn’t misuse the real risk?”Both are real. Losing control doesn’t need a bad actor.
“Regulation will handle it” and “humans always adapt” were the objections our chatbot never resolved, and the most common doubts left at the end: give your best answer here. Also expect, from interviews with AI researchers: “it'll get solved as AI develops” and “we'll test before deployment”. Answer objections head-on: in a public experiment, leaving counterarguments unanswered reversed the movement. Acknowledging good points also helped in our data.
